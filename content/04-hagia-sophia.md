# Hagia Sophia: The Great Church and the Patriarch's Houses

*The Great Church, the patriarch's palace beside it, and the Augustaion square. Boniface claimed all three on 13 April, and the Venetians later took the church for their patriarch.*

## Design note (for you, not for students)

This is the one region you left to me. **The sources tell us a lot about this church and very little, and that contested, about what happened in it during the sack.**

| What the sources say | Source |
|---|---|
| The church was intact, described in detail | Clari ch. 85 |
| Baldwin was anointed at its high altar on 16 May | Clari ch. 96 |
| Altar broken up, mules in the sanctuary, a woman dancing on the synthronon, set out as *report* | Choniates (Mag. 315) |
| Choniates' refuge stood near the church | Mag. 322–23 |
| The fullest inventory of what was taken | Novgorod, per your paper (**P**) |
| Churches keep what they need for worship | The March Pact, per your paper (**P**) |
| The ciborium came down more than a year later | Mag. 357; your paper |
| The Venetians took the church for their patriarch | *Devastatio* |

**So the region is built around evidence.** Every unique scene carries one of three labels in its source note:

| Label | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Attested** | It happened |
| **Contested** | A source says it happened, and another source or a critic doubts it |
| **Counterfactual** | It did not happen in the sack |

The debrief can then put a simple question to the class: *Did what you did here happen?* Each region's scenes can carry the label, but this is the region where it does the most work.

**The woman on the synthronon is not a playable scene.** Your paper's reading, a non-eyewitness report built on Old Testament topoi, and the sexual content of the IM version, together keep it out. She appears in the GM's narration notes as Choniates' report, with your critique attached. If you want her in the game at all, the defensible form is a rumour scene: what do *you* tell people happened in the Great Church? That turns her into a lesson about how a story becomes history. I haven't written it unless you ask.

**Region blurb (student-facing).** The Great Church, Holy Wisdom, is the largest church in Christendom. Last night two Greek noblemen drew lots under its dome for an empire that neither could hold. This morning the marquis Boniface is claiming it.

## S-Q1 · The Doors of the Great Church

*Shared question 1 · Tuesday 13 April, morning · Every choice ends the same way: Boniface's men take the church and the patriarch's houses.*

**Prompt.** Last night, under this dome, two noblemen drew lots for the empire, and the winner fled before dawn. This morning the marquis Boniface claims the Great Church and the patriarch's houses beside it, and his men are coming up the steps. You reach the doors with them. Every hinge and fitting on them is silver. Inside, Greek clergy and townspeople are still at prayer under a hundred hanging lamps.

**A · Violence.** Clear the Greeks out of the church so the marquis's men can take it.
> They go, some of them shoved down the steps. The church is empty and echoing when the marquis's banner comes in. His captain thanks you less warmly than you expected.
`marks 50 · fama -2 · nefas +1`

**B · Piety.** Enter as a pilgrim, kneel, and leave the Greeks to their prayers.
> You kneel among the Greeks under the dome. Nobody understands anyone else's prayers, and for a little while no one minds.
`marks 0 · fama +2 · nefas -1`

**C · Plunder.** Take the offering boxes and the candlesticks by the doors before the guards are posted.
> Coins, wax and silver. You are down the steps again before Boniface's men set the watch.
`marks 150 · fama 0 · nefas 0`

**D · Sacrilege.** Pry the silver plating from the great doors.
> It comes away in strips. The doors of the largest church in Christendom stand bare in places when the marquis arrives, and he wants to know who did it.
`marks 200 · fama -1 · nefas +1`

**Source note.** *Status: attested.*

- **Who took the church.** Clari ch. 80: the marquis took the Palace of the Lion's Mouth, the Minster of St Sophia and the houses of the patriarch.
- **The doors and lamps.** Clari ch. 85: every door, hinge and socket was of silver, and about a hundred great lamps hung down the church.
- **The night before.** Choniates (Mag. 314): after Mourtzouphlos fled, Doukas and Laskaris contested the throne in the Great Church, and Laskaris was chosen by lot.

## S-Q2 · The Healing Pillars

*Shared question 2 · 13–17 April · Every choice ends the same way: you move on into the church.*

**Prompt.** Along the aisles, sick Greeks are still coming to press themselves against certain pillars, which they say cure diseases of the kidneys and the side. By the great door hangs a small metal tube. The Greeks say that if a sick man puts it to his lips, it draws the sickness out of him. A line of the sick waits for its turn.

**A · Piety.** Let them be, and stand by to keep other soldiers away from them.
> The line moves slowly. Nobody is healed while you watch, and nobody is harmed either. An old woman blesses you in a language you don't speak.
`marks 0 · fama +2 · nefas -1`

**B · Sacrilege.** Chip a piece off a healing pillar to take home as a relic.
> It takes a dagger and some effort. The Greeks around you make a noise you won't forget. Whether a chip of stone cures anything in Picardy remains to be seen.
`marks 50 · fama -1 · nefas +1`

**C · Violence.** Drive the sick out. The church belongs to the marquis now.
> They go, the slowest helped along by your boots. In the scramble they leave behind the coins they brought for the clergy.
`marks 50 · fama -2 · nefas +1`

**D · Plunder.** Take the coins and offerings they have brought for the clergy.
> The sick pay you instead of the priests, because you leave them no choice.
`marks 100 · fama -1 · nefas 0`

**Source note.** *Status: attested as belief.*

- **The marvels.** Clari ch. 85: every pillar of the church had some virtue of healing. One cured a man of disease of the reins when he rubbed himself against it, another disease of the side. By the great door hung a pipe that drew sickness out of anyone who put it to his mouth, and held fast the sickest longest.
- **How Clari tells them.** He reports these as the Greeks' claims. The scene treats them as what the sick believed, not as fact.

## S-1 · Position 1 · The High Altar

*Unique question 3 · 13–17 April · Tier: highest.*

**Prompt.** The altar table is a slab of gold and precious stones fused into a single piece, fourteen feet long, the gift of an emperor long ago. A Greek sacristan tells you a story is already going round the city that the Latins have broken it up and shared out the pieces. It is still whole.

**A.** Break it up, and take your share of the gold and stones.
> It takes a crew with hammers, and the gold comes away in pieces. If the story going round the city wasn't true this morning, you have made it true.
`marks 750 · fama -2 · nefas +4`

**B.** Take the jewelled altar cloth, and leave the table.
> The cloth is heavy with gems and gold thread. The table stands bare.
`marks 400 · fama -1 · nefas +2 · prize: The Altar Cloth of the Great Church (sells for the marks shown; a debrief exhibit)`

**C.** Take the gold chalices and patens from the altar.
> The chalices will serve as wine cups at a feast in camp tonight. Choniates said the Latins would do exactly this.
`marks 300 · fama 0 · nefas +2`

**D.** Guard it for the coming coronation.
> A new emperor must be anointed at this altar, and you mean it to be there for him. When Baldwin kneels before it in May, the table is whole.
`marks 0 · fama +3 · nefas -2`

**Source note.** *A: contested. B: contested. C: attested as charge. D: attested as outcome.*

- **The altar intact.** Clari ch. 85: the table on the altar was of gold and precious stones, fourteen feet long, made by a rich emperor.
- **The altar destroyed.** Choniates (Mag. 315) says it was broken into pieces and divided among the despoilers. He frames the passage as report and was not an eyewitness.
- **The altar standing in May.** Clari ch. 96: Baldwin was anointed before the altar on 16 May. Your paper adds that the altar appears again at Henry's coronation, and argues the account is confused or exaggerated.
- **The altar cloth.** Your paper cites the Novgorod chronicle (**P**) for the jewelled altar cloth being taken.
- **The chalices.** Choniates (Mag. 314) charges that the Latins seized chalices and patens and set them on their tables as cups and dishes.
- **Debrief question.** Did A happen? The best evidence says probably not.

## S-2 · Position 2 · The Ciborium

*Unique question 3 · 13–17 April · Tier: high.*

**Prompt.** Over the altar rises a canopy of solid silver, shaped like a bell tower, on silver columns and thickly overlaid with gold: tens of thousands of pounds of metal. A crusader engineer is walking round it, working out how it might be brought down.

**A.** Help him rig ropes and pull it down.
> It is far too big for a day's work, or a week's. By nightfall you have one silver column off its base and a great deal of rope.
`marks 500 · fama -1 · nefas +3`

**B.** Strip whatever gold overlay you can reach.
> You work along the lower edge with a knife. The gold comes off in curls, and the silver beneath shows through like a wound.
`marks 300 · fama -1 · nefas +2`

**C.** Report its weight and worth to the commissioners collecting the booty.
> The clerks write it down with interest. It is too large for anyone to steal quietly, and now they know it is there.
`marks 0 · fama +2 · nefas 0`

**D.** Persuade the engineer to leave it. The church needs its canopy.
> He shrugs and goes looking for easier silver. The ciborium stands over the altar, for now.
`marks 0 · fama +1 · nefas -1`

**Source note.** *A and B: counterfactual for April 1204. C and D: consistent with what happened.*

- **The canopy.** Clari ch. 85: above the altar was a canopy made like a bell tower, of massive silver, on silver pillars.
- **When it came down.** Choniates (Mag. 357, the *De Signis*): the Latins later pulled down the ciborium, which weighed many tens of thousands of pounds of the purest silver and was thickly overlaid with gold. Your paper dates this to more than a year after the sack.
- **Debrief point.** It wasn't looted in the frenzy. It was taken later, methodically.

## S-3 · Position 3 · Mules in the Sanctuary

*Unique question 3 · the weeks after 13 April · Tier: high.*

**Prompt.** The leaders have agreed that the churches may keep what they need for worship and must give up the rest. A party has come to strip the silver from the sanctuary railing, the pulpit and the gates. They want mules and asses brought right up to the altar to carry it away. One beast has already slipped on the polished marble.

**A.** Bring the mules in, and help load the silver.
> The railing comes away in sections, and the mules carry it out through the nave. When the fallen beast won't rise, someone finishes it with a knife where it lies.
`marks 400 · fama -1 · nefas +3`

**B.** Refuse to bring beasts into the sanctuary. Carry the silver out by hand.
> It takes three times as long and wrecks your back. The sanctuary floor stays clean.
`marks 200 · fama +1 · nefas +1`

**C.** Take only what the clergy agree they can spare.
> The Greek sacristan points to what the liturgy cannot do without, and you leave it. The rest goes to the commissioners, as the leaders agreed.
`marks 150 · fama +2 · nefas -1`

**D.** Load your own mule first, and leave before the commissioners count.
> One mule, loaded with silver, goes out a side door before anyone has written anything down.
`marks 350 · fama -2 · nefas +2`

**Source note.** *The stripping: attested. The animals' slaughter: contested. The method, under orders, after the sack: P, from your paper.*

- **Choniates' account.** Mag. 315, not an eyewitness: to remove the silver from the bema railing, the pulpit and the gates, the Latins led mules and asses with packsaddles into the sanctuary. Some slipped on the polished floor and were stabbed, fouling it with blood and dung.
- **Your paper's reading.** Bringing animals in is plausible. The slaughter echoes Old Testament topoi of unclean sacrifice in the Temple.
- **The March Pact** (via Andrea p. 142, per your paper): churches were to keep enough to sustain worship honourably, and the rest was to be divided.
- **Novgorod** (per your paper, **P**): the doors, silver columns, crosses and railings were taken.
- **When it happened.** Your paper concludes the stripping was a methodical campaign in the following weeks, under Boniface's control, not a frenzy. Option A's outcome keeps Choniates' detail as one soldier's act. Option C follows the Pact.

## S-4 · Position 4 · The Patriarch's House

*Unique question 3 · 13–17 April · Tier: middling.*

**Prompt.** The patriarch's palace beside the church stands open. The patriarch has gone, and his household is scattered. His rooms hold vestments stiff with gold thread, a library, chests of documents, and the seals of his office.

**A.** Take the vestments and the plate.
> Gold-thread vestments sell as cloth of gold. The plate sells as silver.
`marks 300 · fama 0 · nefas +2`

**B.** Take the patriarchal seals and archive.
> Whoever settles the Greek church will need to know what the patriarchs held, granted and owed. Now that knowledge is yours to share or to sell.
`marks 0 · fama 0 · nefas +1 · prize: The Patriarch's Seals and Archive (+1 vote on the committee that decides the church settlement)`

**C.** Claim the house for the Latin clergy of the host.
> You chalk the bishops' mark on the door. The Latin clergy are grateful, and they will need somewhere to live.
`marks 0 · fama +2 · nefas 0`

**D.** Guard it until its people come back.
> A few servants creep back over the following days and find their master's rooms as he left them.
`marks 0 · fama +1 · nefas -1`

**Source note.** *Status: the setting is attested; the contents are invented.*

- **Who held it.** Clari ch. 80: the marquis took the houses of the patriarch.
- **Where the patriarch went.** Choniates (Mag. 325–26): the patriarch left the city on a donkey on 17 April. If the Blachernae players capture him (B-Q3), he is in Latin hands instead.

## S-5 · Position 5 · The Keys of the Great Church

*Unique question 3 · May 1204 · Tier: middling.*

**Prompt.** Weeks later, Venetian clergy arrive to take possession of the Great Church. The empire goes to the Franks, they say, so the patriarchate is Venice's. The Frankish clergy refuse to hand over the keys and have appealed to the pope. Both sides want strong arms at the doors.

**A.** Hold the doors for the Venetians.
> The Venetian clergy take the church, as the agreement made in March said they would. They don't forget who stood at the door.
`marks 0 · fama +1 · nefas 0 · prize: Venetian Gratitude (+1 vote on the committee that decides the church settlement)`

**B.** Hold the doors for the Frankish clergy, pending the pope's reply.
> The Frankish clergy keep the keys for now and send their letters to Rome. They don't forget who stood at the door.
`marks 0 · fama +1 · nefas 0 · prize: Frankish Clerical Gratitude (+1 vote on the committee that decides the church settlement)`

**C.** Sell the keys to whichever side pays more.
> Both sides pay you something, and you end up despised by both, which is a common result.
`marks 300 · fama -2 · nefas +1`

**D.** While they quarrel at the doors, slip into the sanctuary for the plate.
> Nobody is watching the altar while everyone is watching the doors.
`marks 250 · fama -1 · nefas +2`

**Source note.** *Status: attested. Not part of the three days.*

- **The dispute.** *Devastatio*: the Venetians occupied the church of the Holy Wisdom, saying the empire was the Franks' and the patriarchate would be theirs. A dispute arose between the Frankish clergy and the Venetians, and the Frankish clergy appealed to the pope.
- **The March agreement.** Clari ch. 68: if the emperor was a Frank, the patriarch would be a Venetian.
- **The outcome.** Choniates (Mag. 357): a Venetian, Thomas, arrived as patriarch.
- **Roster.** Nivelon of Soissons (#4) and Garnier of Troyes (#26) on the Frankish side; the Venetian roster on the other.

## S-6 · Position 6 · The Lamps

*Unique question 3 · 13–17 April · Tier: lowest · Repeatable.*

**Prompt.** A hundred great lamps hang down the nave, each on a silver chain as thick as a man's arm and each holding twenty-five or more little lamps. The Greeks reckon every one is worth two hundred marks. There are plenty to go round.

**A.** Cut one down.
> It comes down with a crash that echoes round the dome. Two hundred marks of silver, and a little less light in the church.
`marks 200 · fama -1 · nefas +1`

**B.** Take a length of the silver chain.
> Easier to carry than a whole lamp, and still silver.
`marks 100 · fama 0 · nefas +1`

**C.** Leave them burning.
> The lamps burn on above you. The Greek clergy who are still here see who left them alone.
`marks 0 · fama +1 · nefas 0`

**D.** Take only the small oil lamps, not the great silver frame.
> A sack of little brass and silver lamps, and the great frame left hanging.
`marks 50 · fama 0 · nefas 0`

**Source note.** *Status: attested.*

- **The lamps.** Clari ch. 85: about a hundred lamps hung down the minster, each on a silver chain as thick as a man's arm, each holding twenty-five or more lamps, and each worth at least two hundred silver marks.
- **Why it can repeat.** It works for two players because Clari counts a hundred of them.
